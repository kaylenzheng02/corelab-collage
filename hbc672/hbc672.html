HBC 672

From NASA / Hubble: Serpens Nebula HBC 672 · Flapping Bat Shadow

Serpens Nebula HBC 672: the Bat Shadow

NASA, ESA, and STScI

Hubble WFC3/IR · Released October 31, 2018 · Also cataloged as [EC 92] 82

Overview

Shadows on Earth can be mysterious, but in space they can reveal what we otherwise could not see. In the Serpens Nebula, nearly 1,300 light-years away, a young star’s shadow play exposes secrets of its unseen planet-forming disk. Hubble’s near-infrared vision captured the shadow cast when the star’s brilliant light is blocked by that disk.

The Bat Shadow

Named HBC 672, this Sun-like star is surrounded by a debris ring of dust, rock, and ice: a disk too small and too distant to see directly, even with Hubble. Like a fly wandering into a flashlight beam, its shadow is projected large upon the cloud in which it was born. In the Hubble image, the feature (nicknamed the “Bat Shadow”) spans approximately 200 times the length of our solar system and appears in the upper right of the frame.

Context

“This is an analog of what the solar system looked like when it was only 1 or 2 million years old,” explained Klaus Pontoppidan (STScI). “For all we know, the solar system once created a shadow like this.” Infrared light makes both the small dusty disk and the much larger nebula easier to study than visible light alone.

Object facts

  • Constellation: Serpens
  • Distance: about 1,000 to 1,300 light-years
  • Image field: ~2 arcmin across (about 0.6 light-years)
  • Instrument: Hubble WFC3/IR · Filters F125W, F164N · Observed 30 August 2018

The flapping motion

Later Hubble analysis showed the Bat Shadow’s “wings” appear to flap. That motion is most likely caused by a saddle-shaped, flared disk with two peaks and two dips. Light takes about 40 to 45 days to travel from the star to the perceivable edge of the shadow.
